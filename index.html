WordMapper

Six everyday words name any point on earth to 34 centimetres. Say fewer for a coarser place — or, if whoever is listening already knows which country you are in, drop the leading word and keep every centimetre.

laptop.tournament.simply.govern.pasta.energy

Big Ben, to 34 cm and checksummed. Every word is graded CEFR A1–B2 — everyday English, nothing you would have to spell out. One grid over the whole earth, so this is the address for that spot; in the UK the first word can go unsaid.

Try it

Open the grid — click anywhere on earth and the address sits on the map, nothing else. The six words appear straight away with no network involved; OpenStreetMap is then asked which country the click landed in, and the words that country can stand in for go grey.

There is one grid

No regional boxes, no scope to choose, nothing to switch. There used to be — a box around Britain and Ireland at four words, another around Australia — and they are gone.

A box has to be a rectangle, and most of the world cannot be boxed without swallowing a neighbour. Africa and Europe interleave across the Mediterranean — Tunisia reaches further north than the southern tip of Spain — so no horizontal line separates them, and when both boxes existed Tunis and Algiers resolved as “Europe”. Australia could be boxed cleanly, but only by cutting at 12° S and giving up Cape York, because Papua New Guinea reaches further south than Australia’s own northern tip.

And every box gave a place a second address. A box was also coarser than the global grid it stood in for: four words over Britain was 2.66 m, against 34 cm for six words anywhere. What the box was really selling was the leading word — and a country supplies that just as well, without a rectangle drawn by hand. That is further down.

Drop leading words: same precision, fewer words

The leading words are the coarse ones, so whoever is listening can supply them if they already know roughly where you are — the way nobody dials +44 or an area code to a neighbour. Dropping words leaves an ambiguity of exactly one tile, and any accuracy better than half a tile pins it down. A leading dot marks the missing words.

The checksum is what makes this safe rather than a leap of faith. Its seven bits cover the whole position, so when the missing coarse words are filled in from a reference point, guessing the wrong tile fails the check 99.2 % of the time. A reference too far away says so, rather than answering confidently with the wrong place.

It falls conveniently over the UK. The whole country spans only a handful of distinct first words, so a British conversation drops the first word almost for free. Not quite free near a seam, where two neighbouring towns start with different words — which is precisely the case the checksum catches.

A country can supply them instead

Filling a dropped word back in needs either a nearby point or a region. A point takes the nearest tile that fits. A region hands the choice to the checksum: every tile inside it is tried, and a wrong one survives only one time in 256. A country is such a region, and OpenStreetMap’s reverse geocoder will hand you its bounding box along with its name.

How many words a window buys is one number: how many candidate tiles it holds. Each word is 10 bits, so one word fewer is 1024 times as many tiles. A length works exactly when no other candidate survives the check — a Poisson zero at rate (tiles − 1)/256. Nothing about countries enters into it; a country is just a box someone else drew.

Shortening this way costs detection, and the cap is what bounds the cost. When a word is misheard the true tile no longer matches, so every candidate in the window becomes a fresh lottery against the same eight check bits: a wrong word is caught only (255/256)k of the time. At the cap of six candidates that is 97.7 %, against 99.61 % for the full address — and over Britain, where the window holds three, it is 100 % in measurement. Uncapped it is far worse: a window the size of the United States holds 318 candidates and falls to 29 %, where most mishearings resolve silently to somewhere else in the country, which is the worst failure there is because it looks like an answer. So no address is shortened above six candidates, and none is read that way either.

Filling the word in from a nearby point has no such loss: it tests the single nearest tile — one chance to be fooled rather than k — and stays at 99.61 %. That asymmetry is the real difference between the two ways of doing it.

The grid never moves. The country is consulted when the address is read, as a search window; it is not part of the address and nothing is bound to it. A border can be redrawn, a territory can change hands, and the words for a place are unchanged. The worst a wrong window can do is cost a word — the full six are always correct, and are shown straight away, before any network call.

Cells are square

Cell aspect is the projected frame’s aspect times a power of two, and the address length fixes the exponent’s parity — so an even bit count can only land on frame × 4k. The standard parallel is therefore chosen to make the projected world exactly square: its aspect is πK², so K = 1/√π gives 1, at 55.654°.

Ten bits to a word makes every length an even bit count, so every length lands exactly square — 706 km, 22.1 km, 689 m, 21.5 m, 67 cm, 34 cm, all of them 1:1. The projection is equal-area, so this changes the shape and not the resolution. At eleven bits to a word the odd lengths paid for it at 2:1; that penalty is simply gone.

The part that is easy to get wrong

Coordinates are interleaved once at full precision and the bit string is truncated. Deriving the interleave order per length looks equivalent and is not — at eleven bits to a word, 33 bits split the axes 17/16 while 22 and 44 split evenly, three unrelated sequences. When I built it that way the three-word address came out entirely different while the two- and four-word ones agreed with each other, so a spot check on 2 and 4 would have passed.

Ten bits to a word happens to make every split even, so the bug could not bite now. The code still interleaves once, because that is what is actually correct rather than what happens to work at one word size. The test suite checks every length over 1,500 points either way.

The fifth word does two jobs

Four words alone reach 5.4 m, which is wide. A whole fifth word of position would reach 8 cm, which is finer than anyone needs. So the fifth word's eleven bits are split between refinement and checksum:

RefineCheckCellWrong word caught
01067 cm99.90 %

3724 cm99.22 %

Shipped at four refine plus seven check. For comparison, what3words is 3 m with no checksum at all — this is finer and verified.

A checksum genuinely cannot live at every length: its bits would sit where the next word's position bits must go, and reserving eight at every length would take two words from 11 km to 176 km. Putting it in the last word costs nothing at the shorter lengths, which are simply unverified. Six words is therefore terminal — a sixth would have to reinterpret the check bits.

The word list

1,024 words, every one graded CEFR A1–B2 — the band a person can retrieve under pressure, not merely recognise. Exactly 10 bits each, with nothing wasted rounding to a word boundary. Generated by tools/wordlist from the 9,025 graded headwords of the Oxford 5000 and the English Vocabulary Profile, minus names, places, offensive and vulgar words, inflections of other words, words spelled two ways — and then everything confusable or sound-alike.

No two words are within one phoneme error of each other. Zero homophones, zero pairs one articulatory feature apart. Substituting, inserting or deleting a phoneme costs 1.0; a confusable substitution (free/three, lamp/ramp) costs 0.5, and every pair on the list is at least 2.0 apart.

BIP-39 was here first and was the wrong list. It is designed to be typed and checksummed, not spoken: it contains pair/pear, peace/piece, right/write and wear/where, and 53 % of its words have a same-or-one-phoneme twin. Its only guarantee is unique four-letter prefixes, which says nothing about what happens down a phone line.

The cost is a word: 1,024 is 10 bits against BIP-39’s 11, so an address is six words rather than five. It is finer for it — six words of 10 bits beats five of 11, 34 cm against 1.35 m.
