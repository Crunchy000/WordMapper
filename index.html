WordMapper

Encoding geographic coordinates as short, memorable word sequences — an experiment in whether a small, carefully chosen vocabulary can beat a large careless one.

The demo

An interactive grid over the UK and Ireland. Click a cell to pick a word; each click subdivides that cell with the same grid, so every extra word refines the address. Three words gets you to about 5.5 × 8 m.

How it works

The bounding box is divided into a 53 × 53 grid. Cells are numbered along a generalised Hilbert curve, so cells that are adjacent in space are adjacent in the numbering. Each index picks a word from a 2,809-word list — exactly one word per cell, so no ground is left unaddressable. No two words are within one edit of each other in spelling or pronunciation, which is what separates sail from sale.

Because each word strictly subdivides the previous cell, every prefix is already a valid coarser address. A truncated address is degraded, not invalid.

Why a small word list

For an area A addressed with L words from a list of N:

cell side = sqrt( A / NL )

Coverage is cheap — resolution scales with the square root of area, so doubling the area costs only 41 % in cell size. The vocabulary and the address length are the exponential levers. Grid dimensions do not appear at all: they change the shape of cells, never their area.

That reframes the usual trade. Reaching 3 m worldwide needs about 38,000 words at three words per address — or about 2,750 at four. what3words took the first route. The second keeps the property that makes a mnemonic list worth having:

Global coverage does not cost resolution. It costs one more word.
