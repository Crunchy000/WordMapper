Open the grid — click anywhere on earth and the address sits on the map with its hexagon drawn round the point. It is computed in the page: there is no country to consult and no word to drop, so all six are always there.

Hexagons, on H3

The grid used to be squares on a cylindrical equal-area projection. They were square in the projection, but on the ground only at 55.65°: a 2.99 m cell was 1.7 × 5.3 m at the equator and 9.7 × 0.9 m at 80°. Hexagons on the same map would have been stretched just as badly.

H3 fixes the map as well as the shape. It lays hexagons on an icosahedron, so a cell is close to a regular hexagon everywhere on earth. Every neighbour is the same distance away, there are no diagonals, and a point is never more than one edge (1.55 m on average) from its cell’s centre. The trade is area: H3 is not equal-area, and resolution-14 cells run from about 3.8 to 7.6 m².

The address is H3’s own hierarchy

A resolution-14 cell is a base cell (one of 122) and fourteen base-7 digits, each choosing one of a cell’s seven children. Each word carries a whole number of those digits plus a digit of checksum:

Base 7 does not pack evenly into 1,296. 74 = 2,401 does not fit, so three digits is the most a word can hold, and the checks multiply out to only 18,252. The square grid managed 82,944. Resolution 13 would have kept a strong check (1 in 104,976), but a 44 m² cell is a house rather than a doorstep. The values no position uses are not wasted: a misheard word that lands on one, or on a pentagon’s missing child, is refused before the checksum runs.

H3 nests approximately: a coarse hexagon, as drawn, does not quite contain all its descendants. So the point is not always inside a shorter address’s outline, but it is always close to its centre, within 1.1 cell radii at every length.

Drop leading words: same precision, fewer words

The leading words are the coarse ones, so whoever is listening can supply them if they already know roughly where you are — the way nobody dials +44 or an area code to a neighbour. The reference point’s nearby H3 cells supply the missing words, and the one candidate nearest it is tested against the checksum. A leading dot marks the missing words.

A tail keeps only its own words’ check. The dropped words take their check digits with them, which is why the checksum is weighted to the last two words: each gives up a digit of position for a check of 26. A reference too far away says so, rather than answering confidently with the wrong place.

There is nothing to shorten against

Filling a dropped leading word back in needs a nearby point, and tests exactly one candidate. There used to be a second way: a region, usually a country, whose bounding box a reverse geocoder would supply. It is gone. Every candidate the box left was a fresh lottery against the same checksum, it made the word count unpredictable, and it needed a network call. What it bought was a single word. Six words, always, is the trade.

The word list

1,296 words, every one graded CEFR A1–B2 — the band a person can retrieve under pressure, not merely recognise. Generated by tools/wordlist from the 9,025 graded headwords of the Oxford 5000 and the English Vocabulary Profile, minus names, places, offensive and vulgar words, inflections of other words, words spelled two ways — and then everything confusable or sound-alike.

No two words are within one phoneme error of each other. Zero homophones, zero pairs one articulatory feature apart. Substituting, inserting or deleting a phoneme costs 1.0; a confusable substitution (free/three, lamp/ramp) costs 0.5, and every pair on the list is at least 2.0 apart.

BIP-39 was here first and was the wrong list. It is designed to be typed and checksummed, not spoken: it contains pair/pear, peace/piece, right/write and wear/where, and 53 % of its words have a same-or-one-phoneme twin.

1,296 was 36 × 36 for the square grid. H3 does not need that, but 1,311 is the ceiling on A1–B2 graded vocabulary, so 1,296 is close to everything the easy band has to give.
