WordMapper

Three everyday words name any place on earth to one hexagon, 531 m on an edge. Nothing else is needed to decode them — no reference point, no network, no database.

film.balloon.tourism

Westminster, around Big Ben: one resolution-8 hexagon of Uber’s H3 grid, 0.74 km² on average. Every word is graded CEFR A1–B2 — everyday English, nothing you would have to spell out.

Try it

Open the grid — click anywhere on earth and the address appears with its hexagon drawn round the point, or type three words to go to them. It is all computed in the page.

Why resolution 8

Three words from a 1,296-word list are 1,296³ ≈ 2.18 billion combinations. H3 has 122 × 78 ≈ 703 million resolution-8 slots, which fits about three times over; resolution 9 would need 4.9 billion. No grid of any shape could do much better: earth split 2.18 billion ways is 0.23 km² each.

H3 is there for shape. It lays hexagons on an icosahedron, so a cell is close to a regular hexagon everywhere on earth, and every neighbour is the same distance away. It is not equal-area: resolution-8 cells vary by about 2:1 across the globe.

Three words, one number

A word cannot hold four of H3’s base-7 digits (74 = 2,401), so one word per level would stop at resolution 7. Instead the three words are one number:

ordinal = base cell × 7⁸ + its eight digits in base 7
value   = ordinal × 3 + check          check = sha256(cell) mod 3
number  = scramble(value)              a permutation of all 1,296³
words   = number in base 1,296

A wrong word

The spare factor of three is all the checking there is room for. A misheard word is refused about 68 % of the time: it lands past the last value that names a cell, on a pentagon’s missing child, or fails the check. The rest decode to a real place.

So the scramble makes those obvious. Without it, neighbouring places would share leading words, and a wrong last word would land a plausible few kilometres away. With it, changing any word sends the address somewhere unrelated. In the tests the median miss is about 9,900 km, and none of about 6,000 landed within 100 km. It is a six-round Feistel network, and 1,296³ is exactly 46,656², so it is a bijection on every three-word combination with nothing left over.

The price: neighbouring places share no words, and there is no shorter form — two words do not name a bigger area.

The word list

1,296 words, every one graded CEFR A1–B2 — the band a person can retrieve under pressure, not merely recognise. Generated by tools/wordlist from the 9,025 graded headwords of the Oxford 5000 and the English Vocabulary Profile, minus names, places, offensive and vulgar words, inflections of other words, words spelled two ways — and then everything confusable or sound-alike.

No two words are within one phoneme error of each other. Zero homophones, zero pairs one articulatory feature apart. With only a light check, the list is the first defence against a misheard word, and the scramble the second.

BIP-39 was here first and was the wrong list. It is designed to be typed and checksummed, not spoken: it contains pair/pear, peace/piece, right/write and wear/where, and 53 % of its words have a same-or-one-phoneme twin.
