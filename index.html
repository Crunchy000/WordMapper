WordMapper

Six everyday words name any point on earth to four and a half metres, and catch a misheard one 99.9995 % of the time. Say fewer for a coarser place — or, if whoever is listening already knows which country you are in, drop the leading word and keep every metre.

Big Ben, to 4.48 m, with a misheard word caught 999,999 times in a million. The sixth word is pure checksum — it moves the position not at all, so the first five are unchanged from when there were only five. Every word is graded CEFR A1–B2 — everyday English, nothing you would have to spell out. One grid over the whole earth, so this is the address for that spot; in the UK the first word can go unsaid.

Try it

Open the grid — click anywhere on earth and the address sits on the map, nothing else. The five words appear straight away with no network involved; OpenStreetMap is then asked which country the click landed in, and the words that country can stand in for go grey.

There is one grid

No regional boxes, no scope to choose, nothing to switch. There used to be — a box around Britain and Ireland at four words, another around Australia — and they are gone.

A box has to be a rectangle, and most of the world cannot be boxed without swallowing a neighbour. Africa and Europe interleave across the Mediterranean — Tunisia reaches further north than the southern tip of Spain — so no horizontal line separates them, and when both boxes existed Tunis and Algiers resolved as “Europe”. Australia could be boxed cleanly, but only by cutting at 12° S and giving up Cape York, because Papua New Guinea reaches further south than Australia’s own northern tip.

And every box gave a place a second address. A box was also coarser than the global grid it stood in for: four words over Britain was 2.66 m, against 4.48 m for five words anywhere — and it bought that with an unchecked address. What the box was really selling was the leading word — and a country supplies that just as well, without a rectangle drawn by hand. That is further down.

Drop leading words: same precision, fewer words

The leading words are the coarse ones, so whoever is listening can supply them if they already know roughly where you are — the way nobody dials +44 or an area code to a neighbour. Dropping words leaves an ambiguity of exactly one tile, and any accuracy better than half a tile pins it down. A leading dot marks the missing words.

The checksum is what makes this safe rather than a leap of faith. It is one of 186,624 values over the whole position, so when the missing coarse words are filled in from a reference point, guessing the wrong tile fails the check 99.9995 % of the time. A reference too far away says so, rather than answering confidently with the wrong place.

It falls conveniently over the UK. The whole country spans only a handful of distinct first words, so a British conversation drops the first word almost for free. Not quite free near a seam, where two neighbouring towns start with different words — which is precisely the case the checksum catches.

A country can supply them instead

Filling a dropped word back in needs either a nearby point or a region. A point takes the nearest tile that fits. A region hands the choice to the checksum: every tile inside it is tried, and a wrong one survives only one time in 186,624. A country is such a region, and OpenStreetMap’s reverse geocoder will hand you its bounding box along with its name.

How many words a window buys is one number: how many candidate tiles it holds. Each word is a base-36 digit on each axis, so one word fewer is 1,296 times as many tiles. A length works exactly when no other candidate survives the check — a Poisson zero at rate (tiles − 1)/186,624. Nothing about countries enters into it; a country is just a box someone else drew.

Shortening this way costs detection, and the cap is what bounds the cost. When a word is misheard the true tile no longer matches, so every candidate in the window becomes a fresh lottery against the same checksum: a wrong word is caught only (1 − 1/186,624)k of the time.

So the cap is derived from the checksum, not written down — the most candidates that still leave 99.5 % of the detection standing. At a 186,624-value check that is 935, where a 144-value check allowed only six. That is the whole reason every country now buys a word: the United States window holds 404 candidates and still catches 99.7 %, where under the old check it fell to 6 % and nearly every mishearing resolved silently to somewhere else in the country.

Filling the word in from a nearby point has no such loss: it tests the single nearest tile — one chance to be fooled rather than k — and stays at 99.9995 %. That asymmetry is the real difference between the two ways of doing it.

The grid never moves. The country is consulted when the address is read, as a search window; it is not part of the address and nothing is bound to it. A border can be redrawn, a territory can change hands, and the words for a place are unchanged. The worst a wrong window can do is cost a word — the full five are always correct, and are shown straight away, before any network call.

Cells are square

Both axes get the same base-36 split at every word, so a cell’s aspect is just the projected frame’s aspect — at every length, full stop. The standard parallel is therefore chosen to make the projected world exactly square: its aspect is πK², so K = 1/√π gives 1, at 55.654°.

So every length lands exactly square — 627 km, 17.4 km, 484 m, 13.45 m and 4.48 m, all of them 1:1. The projection is equal-area, so this changes the shape and not the resolution. Under the old binary layout this needed a greedy bit order and still left the odd lengths at 2:1; that penalty is simply gone.

The part that used to be easy to get wrong

Each word is one base-36 digit of x and one of y, so a shorter address is literally the leading digits of a longer one. The leading digits do not depend on the trailing ones, and there is nothing to truncate.

The binary layout had to interleave the coordinates once at full precision and truncate the bit string. Deriving the interleave order per length looks equivalent and is not — at eleven bits to a word, 33 bits split the axes 17/16 while 22 and 44 split evenly, three unrelated sequences. When I built it that way the three-word address came out entirely different while the two- and four-word ones agreed with each other, so a spot check on 2 and 4 would have passed. Base-36 removes the failure mode rather than testing around it; the test suite still checks every length over 1,500 points.

Every word can carry check, and the checks multiply

A word that splits its cell r × r keeps r² of its 1,296 values for position and has 1296/r² left over. So the splits fix the resolution and the strength of the checksum together:

SPLITS = [36, 36, 36, 36,   3,    1]
CHECKS = [ 1,  1,  1,  1, 144, 1296]   →  CHECK = 186,624

Words one to four split 36 × 36 and have nothing to spare. Word five refines 3 × 3 and spends its other 144 values on check. Word six splits 1 × 1: it moves the position not at all and is nothing but check. That is why it costs no resolution, and why it is worth 1,296× rather than merely more — only the product of the splits sets the cell size, but the checks multiply.

The check digits are assigned least-significant-first, which is the whole of the backwards compatibility: word five gets checksum % 144, exactly what it carried when it was the last word. And 144 divides 186,624, so the five-word check is a genuine prefix of the six-word one rather than a different function that happens to sit near it. Every address already written down is still valid.

What the fifth word could have spent instead

A whole fifth word of position would reach 37 cm, finer than anyone needs, so it splits only 3 × 3. The two multiply out to 1,296, and any square factorisation is available:

Shipped at three refine plus a 144-value check, which the sixth word then multiplies by 1,296. For comparison, what3words is 3 m with no checksum at all — this is comparable and verified to one part in 186,624.

A checksum genuinely cannot live at every length: it would have to occupy the values the next word needs for position, and reserving 144 of a word’s 1,296 at every length would take two words from 17 km to 209 km. Putting it in the last word costs nothing at the shorter lengths, which are simply unverified.

Six words is terminal. A seventh would have nothing left to do: the sixth already spends its entire word on check, so a seventh could only add precision the grid does not need, or a second check symbol — and two check symbols is a different design, one that corrects a wrong word rather than detecting it.

The word list

1,296 words, every one graded CEFR A1–B2 — the band a person can retrieve under pressure, not merely recognise. 36 × 36, so each word is one base-36 digit of x and one of y. Generated by tools/wordlist from the 9,025 graded headwords of the Oxford 5000 and the English Vocabulary Profile, minus names, places, offensive and vulgar words, inflections of other words, words spelled two ways — and then everything confusable or sound-alike.

No two words are within one phoneme error of each other. Zero homophones, zero pairs one articulatory feature apart. Substituting, inserting or deleting a phoneme costs 1.0; a confusable substitution (free/three, lamp/ramp) costs 0.5, and every pair on the list is at least 2.0 apart.

BIP-39 was here first and was the wrong list. It is designed to be typed and checksummed, not spoken: it contains pair/pear, peace/piece, right/write and wear/where, and 53 % of its words have a same-or-one-phoneme twin. Its only guarantee is unique four-letter prefixes, which says nothing about what happens down a phone line.

The list does not have to be a power of two, and dropping that assumption is what pays for the resolution. Binary forced 1,024 words and a 10.8 m cell; 36 × 36 is 1,296 words and 4.48 m, for the same five words said and a stronger check besides. 1,311 is the ceiling on A1–B2 graded vocabulary, so 1,296 is close to everything the easy band has to give — BIP-39’s 2,048 would carry more per word, but the largest phonetically clean list inside the top 10,000 words of English is 976. Four and a half metres is a parking space; going to six words would reach 12 cm if a doorstep were ever needed.
