WordMapper

Six everyday words name any point on earth to three metres, and catch a misheard one 99.9988 % of the time. Say fewer for a coarser place, or drop the leading ones if whoever is listening already knows roughly where you are — but six is the address.

Try it

Open the grid — click anywhere on earth and the address sits on the map, nothing else. The five words appear straight away with no network involved, and nothing is asked of anything: there is no country to consult and no word to drop, so all six are always there.

There is one grid

No regional boxes, no scope to choose, nothing to switch. There used to be — a box around Britain and Ireland at four words, another around Australia — and they are gone.

A box has to be a rectangle, and most of the world cannot be boxed without swallowing a neighbour. Africa and Europe interleave across the Mediterranean — Tunisia reaches further north than the southern tip of Spain — so no horizontal line separates them, and when both boxes existed Tunis and Algiers resolved as “Europe”. Australia could be boxed cleanly, but only by cutting at 12° S and giving up Cape York, because Papua New Guinea reaches further south than Australia’s own northern tip.

And every box gave a place a second address. A box was also coarser than the global grid it stood in for: four words over Britain was 2.66 m, against 2.99 m for six words anywhere — and it bought that with an unchecked address.

Drop leading words: same precision, fewer words

The leading words are the coarse ones, so whoever is listening can supply them if they already know roughly where you are — the way nobody dials +44 or an area code to a neighbour. Dropping words leaves an ambiguity of exactly one tile, and any accuracy better than half a tile pins it down. A leading dot marks the missing words.

There is nothing to shorten against

Filling a dropped leading word back in needs a nearby point: take the candidate nearest it. That tests exactly one candidate, so it costs no detection at all — one chance to be fooled, the same as reading the whole address.

There used to be a second way: a region, usually a country, whose bounding box a reverse geocoder would supply. It is gone, for three reasons that stack up.

It cost detection, which is the one thing worth protecting. A misheard word removes the true tile, so every candidate the box left was a fresh lottery against the same checksum, and a wrong word was caught (1 − 1/82,944)k of the time rather than 1 − 1/82,944. Saying the sixth word costs less than that.

It made the word count inconsistent. The same country gave four words in one place and five in another, depending on where the box edges fell against the tile lattice — and the largest countries sat right on the cap, shortening most of the time but not always. An address whose length you cannot predict is worse than one that is always six words.

And it needed a network call, to a rate-limited service with a usage policy, for a scheme that otherwise works entirely offline. What it bought was a single word. Six words, always, is the trade.

Cells are square

Both axes get the same base-36 split at every word, so a cell’s aspect is just the projected frame’s aspect — at every length, full stop. The standard parallel is therefore chosen to make the projected world exactly square: its aspect is πK², so K = 1/√π gives 1, at 55.654°.

So every length lands exactly square — 627 km, 17.4 km, 484 m, 26.89 m and 2.99 m, all of them 1:1. The projection is equal-area, so this changes the shape and not the resolution. Under the old binary layout this needed a greedy bit order and still left the odd lengths at 2:1; that penalty is simply gone.

The part that used to be easy to get wrong

Each word is one base-36 digit of x and one of y, so a shorter address is literally the leading digits of a longer one. The leading digits do not depend on the trailing ones, and there is nothing to truncate.

The binary layout had to interleave the coordinates once at full precision and truncate the bit string. Deriving the interleave order per length looks equivalent and is not — at eleven bits to a word, 33 bits split the axes 17/16 while 22 and 44 split evenly, three unrelated sequences. When I built it that way the three-word address came out entirely different while the two- and four-word ones agreed with each other, so a spot check on 2 and 4 would have passed. Base-36 removes the failure mode rather than testing around it; the test suite still checks every length over 1,500 points.

Every word can carry check, and the checks multiply

A word that splits its cell r × r keeps r² of its 1,296 values for position and has 1296/r² left over. So the splits fix the resolution and the strength of the checksum together:

SPLITS = [36, 36, 36, 18,   9,    1]
CHECKS = [ 1,  1,  1,  4,  16, 1296]   →  CHECK = 82,944

Words one to three split 36 × 36 and have nothing to spare. Word four splits 18 × 18 and word five 9 × 9, each keeping a little back. Word six splits 1 × 1: it moves the position not at all and is nothing but check. Only the product of the splits sets the cell size, but the checks multiply — which is how the check gets far bigger than any one word could hold.

Check digits are assigned least-significant-first, so each length's check modulus divides the next one's: a shorter address is verified more weakly rather than differently. Five words are checked at 1 in 64, six at the whole 82,944 — and verification starts at five, not before, because the checksum is over the whole position and four words stop one split short of full depth.

Exactly three metres

Word five's split has to divide 36, so on its own it steps 3 → 4 → 6 and the cell steps 4.48 → 3.36 → 2.24 m, straight over 3 m. Landing on it means taking the last factor of two out of word four as well — which is what costs the four-word length, coarsening it from 13.45 m to 26.89 m. That is the honest price of the round number.

Holding words one to four fixed, word five's split is the whole dial — it sets the cell and the check together, and there is no free lunch anywhere on it: halving the cell quarters the check.

Shipped at 9 × 9, for 2.99 m. For comparison, what3words is 3 m with no checksum at all — this is the same resolution and verified to one part in 82,944.

A checksum genuinely cannot live at every length: it would have to occupy the values the next word needs for position, and reserving 144 of a word’s 1,296 at every length would take two words from 17 km to 209 km. Putting it in the last word costs nothing at the shorter lengths, which are simply unverified.

Six words is terminal. A seventh would have nothing left to do: the sixth already spends its entire word on check, so a seventh could only add precision the grid does not need, or a second check symbol — and two check symbols is a different design, one that corrects a wrong word rather than detecting it.

The word list

1,296 words, every one graded CEFR A1–B2 — the band a person can retrieve under pressure, not merely recognise. 36 × 36, so each word is one base-36 digit of x and one of y. Generated by tools/wordlist from the 9,025 graded headwords of the Oxford 5000 and the English Vocabulary Profile, minus names, places, offensive and vulgar words, inflections of other words, words spelled two ways — and then everything confusable or sound-alike.

No two words are within one phoneme error of each other. Zero homophones, zero pairs one articulatory feature apart. Substituting, inserting or deleting a phoneme costs 1.0; a confusable substitution (free/three, lamp/ramp) costs 0.5, and every pair on the list is at least 2.0 apart.

BIP-39 was here first and was the wrong list. It is designed to be typed and checksummed, not spoken: it contains pair/pear, peace/piece, right/write and wear/where, and 53 % of its words have a same-or-one-phoneme twin. Its only guarantee is unique four-letter prefixes, which says nothing about what happens down a phone line.

The list does not have to be a power of two, and dropping that assumption is what pays for the resolution. Binary forced 1,024 words and a 10.8 m cell; 36 × 36 is 1,296 words and 2.99 m at six words, checked to one part in 82,944. 1,311 is the ceiling on A1–B2 graded vocabulary, so 1,296 is close to everything the easy band has to give — BIP-39’s 2,048 would carry more per word, but the largest phonetically clean list inside the top 10,000 words of English is 976. Four and a half metres is a parking space; going to six words would reach 12 cm if a doorstep were ever needed.
